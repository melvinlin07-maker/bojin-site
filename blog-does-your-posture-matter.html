Does Your Posture Matter While You Work?

By Yu-Ting Lan, from Taiwan · 7 min read

A student once spent ten careful minutes releasing tension along her jaw and neck, technique nearly perfect, and left the session feeling only slightly better than when she started. When I watched her work, the answer was obvious within seconds: she'd spent the whole session hunched forward, chin jutting toward the mirror to see more clearly, neck craned in exactly the shape a stiff neck holds. Her hands were undoing tension her posture was actively creating.

So here's the idea before the detail: your posture during a session is part of the technique, not separate from it. Hunching or craning toward a mirror tenses the very neck and jaw tissue you're trying to release, which means you can be doing everything else correctly and still be working against yourself the entire time.

The posture problem almost nobody notices

It's an easy trap to fall into, and an understandable one. You want to see clearly, so you lean in. The mirror is a little low, so you tip your head down or crane your neck forward to meet it. None of this feels wrong in the moment; it feels like getting a better view. But that same forward, craned position is the shape a tense neck naturally settles into, which means you're holding the problem in place with your posture while your hands try to release it with the tool.

Why this specifically fights the neck work

So much of a bojin session ends with work along the jaw and down the neck, easing tension in exactly that area. A hunched, forward-craned posture shortens and tightens the front of the neck the whole time you're doing that work. It's a bit like trying to stretch a muscle while simultaneously flexing it. The hands can be doing everything right and still be fighting a losing battle against a posture that's actively working the opposite direction.

The key idea

Bring the mirror to your eye line instead of bringing your head down to the mirror. It's a small adjustment, but it removes the single most common posture mistake in one move, and it means your neck starts the session already in a neutral position instead of fighting a craned one for the entire time you're working.

What good posture actually looks like

Nothing elaborate. Sit upright, head roughly level rather than tipped forward or down, shoulders relaxed and settled rather than hunched up toward your ears. If you need the mirror closer for a precise spot, move the mirror or lean your whole upper body slightly rather than craning just your neck forward. The goal isn't a rigid, perfect posture; it's simply not starting the session by tensing the area you're about to try to release.

Set up your position before you begin

This takes less than a minute and is worth doing before every session, the same way you'd check your grip or your pressure.

01

Sit upright with your head level

Find a seat facing your mirror where you can sit tall without leaning forward, head level rather than tipped down or craned toward the glass.

02

Bring the mirror to you

Raise or move the mirror so it sits at your eye line, instead of leaning your head down or forward to meet it.

03

Drop your shoulders before you start

Take one breath and let your shoulders settle down and back before you pick up the tool, so you're not beginning the session already tense.

Once your setup is right, the rest of the technique has a much easier job. You're no longer releasing tension with one hand while your posture quietly rebuilds it.

An honest note

Good posture won't add anything the technique itself doesn't already provide; the angle, pressure, and order still do the actual work. What it removes is a self-created obstacle that can genuinely blunt a session's effect without you ever noticing it was there. The result stays gentle and temporary either way. Good posture just means you're not quietly working against your own hands.

Do this kindly. Adjust your setup for comfort, not for a perfect-looking position; a natural, relaxed upright posture is enough. Skip broken or irritated skin, and stop if anything feels sharp. This is gentle self-care, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment; if you have a neck condition, check with your own professional about a position that's comfortable for you.

Before your next session, check your mirror height before you check your technique. Sometimes the tension you're fighting starts in how you're sitting, not in what your hands are doing.

Quick answers

Does my posture while doing bojin actually matter?

Yes. Hunching forward toward a mirror or craning your neck to see better tenses the exact area, your neck and jaw, that the session is trying to release. You can be doing perfect technique with your hands and still be working against yourself with your posture.

What's the right position to sit in?

Upright with your head roughly level, not tipped forward or craned toward the mirror. Bring the mirror closer or raise it to your eye line instead of leaning in to meet it. Your shoulders should be relaxed and down, not hunched up toward your ears.

Why does leaning toward the mirror specifically cause a problem?

It's a very common, very natural instinct to lean in for a clearer view, but it shortens and tightens the front of your neck, the same tissue you're often trying to ease with the neck-finishing part of a session. You end up creating new tension while releasing old tension nearby.

Should I work standing up or sitting down?

Either works, as long as your head stays level and your shoulders stay relaxed. Sitting tends to make an upright, stable position easier to hold for several minutes, which is why most women find it more comfortable for a full session.
