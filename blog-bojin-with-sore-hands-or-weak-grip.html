Sore Hands or a Weak Grip? When to Pause or Adjust Bojin

By Yu-Ting Lan, from Taiwan · 5 min read · Updated October 15, 2026

You came for a few quiet minutes for your face. Instead, you find yourself thinking about your thumb: how tightly you are holding the tool, whether your hand will manage another pass, whether you should just finish. You do not have to prove you can keep going.

If holding a Bojin stick makes your hand hurt, put it down. A comfortable adjustment may be worth discussing, but pain, numbness, or an unfamiliar loss of strength should not be explained away as a technique mistake.

Comfort and control come before completing the routine

This page is for deciding whether a small manual self-care practice is manageable for you. It cannot identify why your hand hurts or prescribe a grip for arthritis, an injury, or a nerve problem. If you already have a hand-care plan, follow that plan first.

There is a middle ground between clamping the handle and holding it so loosely that it slips. You need comfortable control. A tool that feels heavier than you can guide is a reason to stop, not a reason to let its weight press into your face.

You can leave it here today

The routine is optional. A missed session is not something you need to make up with longer practice or extra pressure tomorrow.

What general joint-care advice can tell us

Mayo Clinic's joint-protection guide for hand arthritis describes the strain of tight thumb-and-finger pinching and how larger or cushioned handles can help with some everyday tasks.

That supports considering the effort a handle asks of your hand. It does not validate a specific Bojin grip or prove a facial tool is suitable for arthritis. A hand therapist can help decide whether an adaptation is useful for your activity. Do not assume that a heavier tool, a flat stone, or a homemade padded handle is automatically easier or safer near the eyes.

If you can handle a tool comfortably, take breaks and avoid a sustained hard squeeze. You do not need to hold it for a set number of minutes to complete a session. For a demonstrated basic hold, see the handle guide; any general instruction still needs to fit what your own hand can manage.

A five-step comfort check, not a hand exercise

  1. Decide whether today is a practice day If your hand is painful, numb, or newly weak, put the tool aside. Do not use a facial routine to test an unexplained hand symptom.
  2. Set up before picking anything up Sit where you can see comfortably and place the tool on a stable surface within easy reach. Support your arm if that feels comfortable, without forcing your wrist into a position.
  3. Check control away from your face Only if handling the tool is comfortable and appropriate for you, hold it over the stable surface. You need enough control that it will not slip, without squeezing hard. If you cannot find that balance, stop.
  4. Keep any practice small and optional Use only a placement you have been shown and can control. Put the tool down before tiredness turns into strain. There is no fixed number of seconds you need to complete, and no need to add neck work to finish.
  5. Choose the next step from how your hand feels If symptoms return or handling remains difficult, stop experimenting with grips and seek appropriate advice. Watching a lesson without practicing, or skipping the routine, is a valid choice.

What about fingertips or the other hand?

They may be options to discuss or learn, not ways to get around pain. An unfamiliar hand can have less control, and using fingertips still asks your finger joints to work. This guide does not establish that a finger technique has the same reach or effect as a tool.

If your own professional has suggested a modification, ask for the specific hold or movement rather than guessing from a group photograph. When a grip adjustment is not the next step

The NHS hand-pain guidance recommends professional advice when pain interferes with normal activities, worsens or keeps returning, or comes with tingling or loss of sensation. Those are reasons to seek an assessment rather than keep changing your hold.

Seek urgent medical assessment for severe hand pain, inability to move the hand or hold things, loss of feeling, or a change in hand shape or color. Use the urgent-care service appropriate to where you live. Do not let a facial routine delay care.

These are general hand-symptom boundaries, not a diagnosis. Facial Bojin is not presented here as treatment for arthritis, a way to restore grip strength, or a substitute for rehabilitation. Also keep the separate facial self-care safety limits in mind, including your skin condition and recent procedures.

If your hand feels comfortable and your question is about contact on the face, continue to comfortable pressure. If it is about which hand to use, read control and hand choice.

Quick answers

Can I do Bojin if I have arthritis in my hands?

A general article cannot clear a repeated grip for your condition. Ask your clinician or hand therapist whether the activity fits your hand-care plan and whether an adaptation is appropriate. A different hold is not permission to work through pain.

What should I do if my thumb hurts while holding the stick?

Put the tool down rather than making it press with its own weight. Do not assume thumb pain is only a grip mistake. If pain keeps returning, interferes with everyday tasks, or comes with tingling or altered sensation, seek an assessment.

Should I switch to my fingers or my other hand?

Only consider a demonstrated option you can control comfortably and that fits any advice you have received. Fingers and the other hand can also become sore. This guide does not establish that a substitute has the same depth or effect as a tool.

Does facial Bojin treat hand arthritis or improve grip strength?

This guide does not establish either benefit. Facial self-care is not a hand rehabilitation program. Follow a condition-specific plan from an appropriate professional rather than using a facial tool as a hand exercise.

When should I seek urgent help for hand symptoms?

Seek urgent medical assessment for severe pain, inability to move the hand or hold objects, loss of feeling, or a change in hand shape or color. A facial routine should not delay care. Persistent or recurring pain also deserves professional advice.
