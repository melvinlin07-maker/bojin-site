How to Hold a Bojin Stick: Comfortable Grip and Control

By Yu-Ting Lan, from Taiwan · 5 min read

You pick up the tool, try to remember the angle, and notice your fingers tightening before you have even started. It is easy to treat that as another thing to get right. A more useful starting question is simpler: can you hold it comfortably, guide it, and stop when you intend to?

For Bojin, a secure, comfortable hold matters more than copying an exact finger position. A pencil-like hold is one option to explore, not a rule every hand must fit. This guide is about handling the tool; it is not a treatment for hand pain or a complete face routine.

Comfortable does not mean barely holding on

You do not need to deliberately clench the handle. You also do not want it balanced so loosely that it can slip toward your face. Aim for enough support to keep control while your fingers and wrist feel comfortable.

The useful check

Can you guide a small movement and stop it without a painful squeeze? Check that away from your face first. More force against your skin is not a way to steady an insecure hold.

A pencil-like hold is a starting point

If it feels natural, try supporting the handle with your thumb and fingers as you would a pen. The exact contact points will vary with the handle shape and your hand. You do not have to press the handle deep into the web of your thumb or hold it at a fixed distance from the tip.

If copying that shape hurts, do not keep pinching until it looks right. Mayo Clinic notes that thumb-and-finger pinching can stress already painful joints in people with hand arthritis. Its guidance concerns everyday joint protection; it does not establish a recommended Bojin grip or show that Bojin is suitable for arthritis.

Finger position and tool angle are separate choices

Where you hold the handle affects how it feels to steer. The working end, the placement, and the instruction you are following also matter. There is no universal rule that every broad-edge movement needs a farther-back grip or every tip placement needs your fingers close to the skin.

Put the tool down before changing your hold or switching ends. Keep your wrist within a comfortable range, and choose a position where you can see and control the working end. This article does not teach eye-area placement; do not infer a safe placement near the eye from the photograph alone.

Five checks before tool practice

These are handling checks, not a facial massage sequence. Do them with the tool away from your face before deciding whether to practise a demonstrated placement.

  1. Start away from your face Sit where you can see the tool clearly. Check your hands and the handle are clean and dry according to the tool care instructions. Do not start if your hand is painful or control feels unreliable.
  2. Find a secure, comfortable hold Try a pencil-like hold only if it suits your hand and the handle. Let your fingers support the tool without forcing a hard pinch. Keep the working end clear of your fingers.
  3. Check whether you can stop With the tool away from your face, make a small, slow movement and stop it deliberately. If it slides, wobbles, or needs a painful squeeze, put it down and reassess.
  4. Reposition with the tool down Set the tool down before changing finger placement or switching ends. Pick it up again and repeat the control check. Do not twist your wrist into discomfort to copy an angle.
  5. Keep the option to stop Use only a placement you have been shown and can control comfortably. Stop if your hand hurts, cramps, tingles, or loses control. A shorter session is optional; finishing a routine is not required.

If the handle feels slippery

Put it down before trying to fix the problem. Product transferred onto your fingers or handle may make it harder to hold. Follow the tool care instructions to clean and dry it; keep skin preparation separate from the handle.

If the working end catches during a gliding movement, that is a different question from a slippery handle. Read dry or with oil: slip and skin comfort. Adding more oil does not automatically solve either problem.

If your hand aches, the answer is not simply “loosen more”

Stop and put the tool down if holding it causes pain, cramping, tingling, or unreliable control. Those symptoms are not proof of a technique mistake. There is no promised five- or ten-minute period during which a correct grip should feel effortless.

If symptoms recur or affect everyday tasks, ask a healthcare professional about your hand. For more detail on when to pause and seek help, read Bojin with sore hands or a weak grip. You can leave tool practice out of your routine.

What this grip guide can help with

The goal is to make handling less of a puzzle: a comfortable hold, a clear view of the tool, and the ability to stop. These practical checks do not establish that a particular grip reaches deeper tissue, changes facial structure, or produces better results.

Keep control and comfort together. Do not practise on irritated or injured skin, press on the eyeball, or continue through hand or skin pain. Bojin is optional self-care, not diagnosis, treatment, or hand rehabilitation.

Sources and scope

  • Mayo Clinic: joint protection for people with hand arthritis — background on painful pinching and everyday handles, not evidence for a Bojin grip.
  • NHS: pain in the back of the hand — when recurring pain, tingling, or loss of sensation needs assessment. It does not diagnose symptoms during Bojin.

For the next part of handling, read the angle of the tool and the supporting hand. Use those guides alongside a demonstrated technique, rather than trying to reconstruct a full routine from grip advice.

Quick answers

How tightly should I hold a Bojin stick?

Use enough grip to keep the tool secure and stop it where you intend, without deliberately clenching. A hold that feels loose but allows slipping is not a useful target. There is no single grip strength that fits every hand or tool.

Is a pencil grip the only correct hold?

A pencil-like hold can be a starting point if it is comfortable and secure. Finger placement depends on your hand, the handle, and the demonstrated technique. Do not force a painful thumb-and-finger pinch to match a photograph.

Should I hold closer to the tip?

There is no fixed distance for every technique. Put the tool down before repositioning your fingers, keep them clear of the working end, and check control away from your face. Follow the instruction for the particular placement.

What if the handle keeps slipping?

Put the tool down. Check that your hands and handle are clean and dry, following the tool care instructions. Do not compensate with a painful grip or more pressure against your face. If secure control is still difficult, skip tool practice.

Does hand cramping mean my grip is wrong?

Not necessarily. Cramping or pain cannot be diagnosed from grip alone. Stop the session rather than repeatedly adjusting and continuing. Recurring symptoms, tingling, or changes in grip deserve advice from a healthcare professional.
