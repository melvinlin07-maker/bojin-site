Bojin Tool Angle: Read the Demonstration Without Chasing Depth

By Yu-Ting Lan, from Taiwan · 5 min read

You pause the video and look at the tool in your own hand. The handle seems to point the same way, but you still cannot tell whether the same part is touching your skin. That is a useful moment to stop and look again. You do not need to find the answer by pressing or tilting until something feels stronger.

In Bojin, angle describes the tool's orientation at a particular placement. It is a handling detail to understand in context, not a dial for choosing a tissue layer. Start by seeing the contact surface clearly, alongside the placement, movement, and pressure.

Look at the contact surface before the handle

The handle is easy to see, so it is tempting to copy its direction. But a curved edge, a broad face, and a rounded tip can meet the skin differently even when the handle appears similar. Two differently shaped tools may not share the same contact just because you hold their handles at the same tilt.

When watching a lesson, first locate the part touching the skin. Then notice where it is placed and whether it moves. If a hand or camera angle hides that information, a still image is not enough to reconstruct the technique.

What “flat,” “tilted,” and “upright” can tell you

  • Flat can describe a surface lying close to the skin. The word alone does not establish a warming or drainage effect.
  • Tilted describes a change in orientation. It does not tell you which tissue layer is being reached or whether the contact suits a particular placement.
  • Upright describes the direction of the tool. It is not permission to press a tip into a sensitive area or to drag a raised corner across the skin.

These words are useful for discussing a demonstration. They are incomplete instructions on their own.

The question to ask

“Can I see which surface touches, where it sits, and how it moves?” is more useful than “Have I reached 45 degrees?” If those details are unclear, pause the practice.

Why there is no universal 30–45 degree rule here

A degree measurement needs a reference surface. Your face curves, the tool may curve, and the view through a camera can hide the actual contact. A single number cannot replace the placement-specific instruction.

This guide does not establish an angle that reaches fascia, releases a knot, moves fluid, or changes facial structure. A feeling of resistance does not verify any of those effects. There is no need to tilt higher until the tool feels as though it has caught something.

Five checks for following an angle demonstration

Use these to review what you are seeing before practice. They are not a sequence of facial strokes.

  1. Identify the working surface Watch which part actually touches the skin: a broad face, a curved edge, or a rounded tip. Do not infer this only from the direction of the handle.
  2. Check the view and placement Find the contact point, the direction of movement, and whether it is a glide or a stationary placement. If the camera or your hand hides them, stop and seek a clearer demonstration.
  3. Check handling away from the face With the tool away from your face, see whether you can hold the demonstrated orientation and stop a small movement comfortably. Put it down before repositioning your fingers or changing ends.
  4. Keep pressure separate from angle Only practise a placement that is clear and suitable for you, beginning with light, controlled contact. Do not raise the edge or add force because the sensation seems faint.
  5. Lift away before reviewing Stop if contact catches, stings, hurts, or drags the skin. Lift the tool away before reviewing the demonstration. Do not keep testing an uncomfortable spot.

If the movement catches, stop before adjusting

Lift the tool away rather than rotating an edge while it is loaded against your skin. Put it down if you need to change your grip or working end. Review comfortable handling and control before trying to copy the orientation again.

For gliding contact, catching may also call for reviewing preparation. Read slip and skin comfort. Stinging or irritation is a reason to stop, not an instruction to add oil and keep experimenting.

Angle and pressure need separate attention

Changing the tilt is not a substitute for keeping force light and controlled. Equally, comfortable pressure does not tell you that an unclear placement is appropriate. Review how much pressure to use without chasing a stronger sensation.

A rounded tip does not make every upright placement suitable. This page does not teach eye-area, nose-side, or deep neck techniques. Do not improvise those placements from the tool shape or the lesson photograph.

What a photograph cannot show

The photograph above shows a supervised teaching setting. It is not an angle diagram, a measurement of force, or a complete set of instructions for practising on yourself. A short video gives more context, but cannot measure the pressure in your hand or assess your skin.

Keep the option to pause. Skip injured, irritated, swollen, or painful skin and keep off the eyeball. If you have a skin condition, take blood thinners, bruise easily, or have had a recent facial procedure, ask your clinician before practice. Comfort alone is not medical clearance.

Source and scope

Cleveland Clinic's guide to gua sha discusses gentle facial contact, irritation, and precautions. Bojin is distinct from gua sha. That source does not validate Bojin angles, a tissue-depth map, or the effects of a particular tilt. The checks here are practical ways to review instruction, not a clinical protocol.

Continue with comfortable grip and control, then pressure and when to stop. If your hand is sore or control feels unreliable, first read when to pause or adjust.

Quick answers

Is 30 to 45 degrees the correct angle for every Bojin technique?

No. This guide does not establish a universal angle. Tool shape, the contact surface, the placement, and the demonstrated movement matter. A number without those details is not enough to copy a technique.

Does tilting the tool reach a deeper layer?

You cannot identify a tissue layer from the tilt or the intensity of a sensation. This guide does not establish that a particular angle releases fascia, drains fluid, or produces a better cosmetic result.

Should I tilt more if I feel very little?

No. A faint sensation is not a reason to raise the edge, add force, or repeat the movement until it catches. Pause and review the demonstrated contact surface and placement instead.

Can I use the rounded tip upright near my eyes or nose?

A rounded tip is not automatic clearance for an upright placement. Do not infer an eye-area or nose-side technique from a photograph or a general angle description. Keep off the eyeball and skip any placement you cannot clearly understand and control.

What should I do if the edge catches or stings?

Lift the tool away and stop the movement. Do not keep it against your skin while searching for a different angle. Review handling and skin preparation away from the face; leave an irritated or painful area alone.
