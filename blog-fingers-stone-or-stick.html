Fingers, a Gua Sha Stone, or a Bojin Stick: What to Use

By Yu-Ting Lan · 5 min read

One of the first things people ask me is what they need to buy before they can start. My favorite answer to give is: nothing. The most important tool for bojin is already attached to your hands. Everything else, the gua sha stone in your drawer, a proper bojin stick, is there to help you hold what your fingers already teach you. Here's what each one does best, so you can start tonight and add to it only when you actually want to.

Start tonight with clean fingertips

Your fingertips are a wonderful first tool, and I mean that seriously, not as a consolation prize. They're sensitive, so they read your face as you go. They can't slip or press too hard by accident. And they cost nothing, so there's no reason to wait.

Wash your hands, add a few drops of facial oil or a little moisturizer so you're not dragging the skin, and use the pads of your fingers to work in the order, angle, and comfortable pressure the method asks for. For the delicate skin around the eyes especially, fingers are often the kindest choice of all. If you learn nothing else but how to do this well with your hands, you already have the method.

The gua sha stone you already own works too

If you bought a gua sha stone once, whether jade, rose quartz, or something else, don't feel you have to replace it. It makes a perfectly good tool for bojin. The trick is that bojin asks you to use it differently: less long, broad gliding, more precise, deliberate work in the right spots.

Use the smooth, rounded edge of the stone, keep a little oil on your skin so it glides, and bring the same three things to it every time: order, angle, position. The stone is just the object in your hand. What turns it into bojin is the method you bring, and that travels with you no matter what you're holding.

The tool made for the method: a bojin stick

The tool the method was built around is a bojin stick: a slim, polished stainless steel tool, shaped to follow the curves of the face. It has two working ends. A broad, smooth edge for larger areas like the forehead, cheeks, and neck, and a small rounded tip for delicate spots such as around the eyes.

Why stainless steel rather than a stone? A few practical reasons. It's easy to keep clean, it doesn't chip or wear down over the years, and it uses no animal materials. What a proper stick really adds is a shape made for the job. Its broad edge suits the big planes of the face and its narrow tip sits in the small, careful spots, so it holds the right angle for you once you know the method. It isn't a stronger version of anything, and it isn't better than the stone in your drawer. It's a tool shaped for this one use, and the method is still what does the work.

  1. Match the end to the area Broad edge for the big planes of the forehead, cheeks, and neck. Small rounded tip for careful spots like the brow and around the eyes.
  2. Always add a little slip A few drops of oil or a layer of cream, every time, whatever tool you're holding. Nothing should ever drag on dry skin.
  3. Keep it clean Wipe your tool before and after. Stainless steel makes this easy, which is part of why the method uses it.
  4. Let the tool hold the angle, not the force A tool's job is to hold a precise angle, not to let you press harder. Keep the pressure comfortable and let the shape do the guiding.
The key idea

The tool matters far less than the method behind it. Fingers, your gua sha stone, or a bojin stick can all carry the same order, angle, and comfortable pressure. Start with what you have; add a stick when you want more precision, not because you need permission to begin.

So which should I use?

Think of it as a gentle progression, not a decision you have to get right on day one. Start with your fingers to learn the feel of the method. Reach for the gua sha stone in your drawer when you want a firmer edge for the cheeks or jaw. Add a bojin stick when you're ready for the extra precision and want the angle held for you. There's no wrong door, and no purchase you must make to feel a real difference tonight.

What can I expect?

Whatever you hold, expect the same gentle, temporary result: a face that looks a little calmer and more rested after a few quiet minutes. A better tool won't give you a dramatically different outcome. What changes the result is doing the method well: the right order, angle, position, and a comfortable pressure. The tool only helps you hold those.

And to be clear: this is gentle self-care, not medical care, no matter what's in your hand. No tool makes it a treatment, and none can do what a needle or a procedure does. It's a calm daily habit for the everyday tension we carry, and that's a lovely thing for it to be.

Do this kindly. Whatever tool you use, keep the pressure comfortable and add a little oil or cream so nothing drags. Never force it or pull the skin, skip any broken or irritated skin, stay off the eyeball, and stop if anything hurts. This is a gentle wellness ritual, not medical advice, diagnosis, or treatment.

So don't let "I don't have the right tool" keep you from starting. You already have the best one. Begin with your hands tonight, add a stone or a stick when it feels right, and let the method, not the object, do the quiet work.

Quick answers

Do I have to buy a bojin stick to start?

Not at all. You can begin tonight with clean fingertips, and they'll teach you the method beautifully. A gua sha stone you already own works too. A bojin stick is something to add later, when you want the extra precision and the right angle held for you, not a barrier to starting today.

Can I just use the gua sha stone I already have?

Yes, and it's a lovely place to start. Use the smooth, rounded edge of your jade or rose quartz stone, add a little oil so it glides, and apply the same order, angle, and comfortable pressure you'd use with any tool. The stone is the tool; the method is what you bring to it.

Why is the bojin stick stainless steel and not a stone?

A few practical reasons. Stainless steel is easy to keep clean, it doesn't chip or wear down over time the way a stone can, and it uses no animal materials. It's shaped with a broad edge and a small rounded tip so it can follow the face and hold a precise angle, which is the whole point of a dedicated tool.

Is a more expensive tool better?

No. The tool matters far less than the method behind it. A careful few minutes with clean fingertips beats an expensive tool used without order, angle, and the right pressure. Spend your attention on the technique first; the tool is only there to help you hold it.
