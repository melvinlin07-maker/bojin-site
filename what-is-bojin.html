The short answer

Facial bojin (撥筋, bō jīn) is a face and neck self-care method taught by Taiwan-based instructor Yu-Ting Lan. You work one small area at a time, in small, complete circles with the rounded tip of a stainless steel bojin stick or your fingertips, about a minute per side, while your other hand steadies the skin. Pressure stays comfortable. It is gentle self-care, not medical care, and not for broken skin, the eyelid itself, or recently injected areas.

Tip ~3–5 mm rounded steel Time about 1 min per area, per side Passes three Start angle low, ~30°–45° (class guideline) Rule comfortable, never painful

What does “bojin” mean?

“Bojin” comes from the Mandarin 撥筋 (bō jīn). Bō means to part something aside with a small, deliberate motion, and jīn means the body’s sinews and soft tissue. You’ll also see it written bo jin, and English speakers often hear it as “butin gua,” which has nothing to do with gua sha beyond the sound. On this site the word means one thing: the method Yu-Ting Lan teaches.

What do you actually learn?

Yu-Ting teaches three choices you make on every stroke: order (which area comes first), angle (how the tool meets the skin) and position (the exact spot, plus the spots you leave alone). A beginner area takes about a minute per side: small, complete circles moving outward, three passes, with the supporting hand keeping the skin from dragging. These are teaching guidelines from her classes, not a clinical dose.

Pressure: both extremes are wrong

Too heavy can bruise. Too light never reaches the tension. Yu-Ting teaches the middle: enough to reach the tension, never enough to hurt. If it hurts or bruises, stop; pain doesn’t mean it “went deeper.” See the safety guide.

Side by side

Is bojin the same as gua sha?

No. Facial gua sha usually sweeps the broad edge of a flat stone in long strokes across a wide area. Bojin works one small spot at a time with a rounded steel tip, in short, complete circles, with a supporting hand. Think of gua sha as smoothing a whole sheet and bojin as easing one fold. They do different jobs, and neither should bruise your face. Advice written about gua sha, such as Cleveland Clinic’s facial gua sha article, doesn’t automatically apply to bojin.

Your tool

What is a bojin stick, and do you need one to start?

A bojin stick is a slim, polished stainless steel tool, about 11.4 cm (4.5 in) long and about 43 g, with two working ends: a broad, smooth edge for the forehead, cheeks and neck, and a small rounded tip, roughly 3–5 mm across, for one precise spot along the brow, temple or jaw angle. Steel is easy to clean and doesn’t chip. You don’t need one to begin: clean fingertips teach direction and comfort first. Never use the tip on the eyelid or eyeball.

Try it tonight

How do you try bojin tonight with just your fingertips?

You can try the same step tonight with no tool, in about a minute for one side, using the pads of your index and middle fingers and a little oil or cream. Stop if anything stings, aches or reddens.

Prepare

Clean hands, a little oil or cream, a mirror in even light. Rest your supporting hand flat on the forehead, just above the brow you’ll work.

About 15 seconds

Circle outward

Small, complete circles from between the brows to the tail. Lift off at the tail and return to the start. Three passes, light and slow.

About 30 seconds

Lift & compare

Hands down. Look at both brows and both upper lids. Notice whether the worked side feels lighter or looks more open.

About 15 seconds

Before you go further

Why fingertips stop at the surface

Facial skin is thin: about half a millimeter on the upper eyelid and roughly 1–2 mm across the forehead and cheeks, measured by high-frequency ultrasound (Ha et al., Plast Reconstr Surg, 2005). A light fingertip stroke mostly moves that top layer, which is why it feels pleasant and is safe to learn on. The muscles that hold your expression sit underneath, and pressing harder only drags thin skin. That’s why the method teaches placement and order instead of more force. Does one side of your face hold more tension? Try the 10-second mirror check

Stand at a mirror in even light, relax your face, and spend about 10 seconds on each check below, comparing left with right. Tick any card where you see a clear difference. This is a self-care observation, and some asymmetry is normal. A difference that appears suddenly, or comes with numbness, weakness or pain, needs a doctor today.

Straight talk

What can’t bojin do?

Bojin is self-care, not medical care. It won’t remove wrinkles, erase under-eye bags, or do what Botox or filler does, and it isn’t a stronger form of gua sha. Some people say their face feels less held for a while afterward; that’s a personal experience, not a structural change. Skip it on broken, sunburned or inflamed skin, after recent injections or facial surgery, and ask your doctor first if you take blood thinners.

Common questions

Bojin questions women ask most

Is “bo jin” or “butin gua” the same thing as bojin?

Yes. They are spelling and hearing variants of the same word, 撥筋 (bō jīn). Bo jin is the usual romanization. Butin gua is how it often sounds to English speakers, and it has no connection to gua sha beyond the sound. On this site, bojin always means the face and neck method taught by Yu-Ting Lan.

Do I need a bojin stick, and what should it look like?

Not to start. Fingertips teach direction and comfort first. If you buy one later, look for polished stainless steel, about 11 cm long, with a broad smooth edge, a small rounded tip of roughly 3–5 mm, and no sharp seams. The tool can't choose placement or pressure for you.

Is bojin safe? Who should skip it?

With comfortable pressure, most healthy adults find it gentle. Skip it during a rosacea flare and on irritated skin, follow your provider's timing after injections or surgery, and never use the tip on the eyelid or eyeball. Pain, bruising or numbness means stop.

How often should I do bojin, and for how long?

Yu-Ting's classes start beginners at about a minute per area, per side, two or three times a week, with rest days between. That's a class starting point, not a clinical dose. Skip a session if your skin feels tender, and judge by comfort and a side-by-side mirror comparison, not by redness.
