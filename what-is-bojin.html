The short answer

Facial bojin (撥筋, bō jīn) is a precise, one-spot face and neck self-care method taught by Taiwan-based instructor Yu-Ting Lan. Instead of sweeping a wide stone across the cheek like gua sha, you work one small area at a time: small, complete circles with a rounded stainless steel tip (about 3–5 mm across) held at a low 30°–45° angle, about a minute per zone (three passes), while your other hand steadies the skin. Pressure stays comfortable. It is gentle self-care, not medical care, and it is not for broken skin, the eyelid itself, or recently injected areas.

Tip ~3–5 mm rounded steel Angle low, ~30°–45° Time about 1 min per zone Rhythm 2–3 short sessions a week Rule comfortable, never painful

What does “bojin” mean, and where does it come from?

“Bojin” — also written bo jin, and sometimes misheard as “butin gua” — comes from the Mandarin 撥筋 (bō jīn). Bō means to move or part something aside with a small, deliberate motion; jīn refers to the body’s sinews and soft tissue. In Taiwan it describes careful hands-on work with that tissue. On this site the word means one specific thing: the face-and-neck self-care method taught by Yu-Ting Lan, founder of Héhé Studio. The name itself is not evidence of any effect; what matters is the sequence, placement, and comfortable pressure you learn.

What do you actually learn in the Bojin Method?

The Bojin Method teaches three choices you make on every stroke: order (which area comes first), angle (how the tool meets the skin), and position (the exact spot, plus the spots you leave alone). A typical beginner zone takes about a minute: small, complete circles moving outward, repeated for three passes, with the supporting hand keeping the skin from dragging. These are teaching parameters drawn from Yu-Ting’s classes, not a clinically validated dose, and you adjust them to comfort. More pressure never makes a routine better.

The one thing everyone gets wrong: pressure

A light touch is the right touch while you learn contact and direction, especially near the eyes. If contact hurts, leaves bruising, or irritates your skin, stop. Those reactions are not proof that the method “reached deeper.” The safety guide explains when to skip hands-on face care altogether.

Side by side

Is bojin the same as gua sha?

No. Gua sha and bojin share roots but teach different things. Facial gua sha usually sweeps the broad edge of a flat stone in long strokes across a wide area. Bojin works one small spot at a time with a rounded steel tip, using short, complete circles, a low 30°–45° starting angle, and a supporting hand. Think of gua sha as smoothing a whole sheet and bojin as easing one fold. Neither should bruise the face. Guidance about gua sha, such as Cleveland Clinic’s facial gua sha article, does not automatically apply to bojin.

Why your face feels held

Why does your face feel tight after a day of screens?

Long screen sessions change how your face holds itself. The American Academy of Ophthalmology notes that people normally blink about 15 times a minute but only about 5–7 times a minute at a screen. While you focus, the orbicularis oculi (the ring muscle around the eye) narrows into a slight squint, the corrugator draws the brows together, the frontalis lifts heavy lids, and the suboccipital muscles at the base of the skull hold a forward head. Hours of this can leave the brow feeling heavy by evening. This describes common held-tension patterns, not a medical assessment; eye pain or vision changes belong with an eye doctor.

Early research measured the same drop: office workers blinked about 22 times a minute when relaxed and about 7 times a minute reading on a screen (Tsubota & Nakamori, N Engl J Med, 1993; see also Patel et al., 1991). The American Academy of Dermatology adds that contracting the same facial muscles for years contributes to lines, which is why it suggests sunglasses to cut down on squinting. Bojin does not change any of that biology. What it offers is a few quiet minutes to notice where you are holding, and to let it go.

Your tool

What is a bojin stick, and do you need one to start?

A bojin stick is a slim, polished stainless steel tool about 11.4 cm (4.5 in) long and about 43 g, with two working ends: a broad, smooth edge for the forehead, cheeks, and neck, and a small rounded tip, roughly 3–5 mm across, for one precise spot along the brow, temple, or jaw angle. Steel is easy to clean and doesn’t chip. You do not need one to begin: clean fingertips teach direction and comfort first, and the full course starts with a no-tool fingertip module. Never use the tip on the eyelid or eyeball.

Try it tonight

How do you try bojin tonight with just your fingertips?

You can try the same pattern tonight with no tool, in about 1 minute for one side. Use a few drops of oil or cream so nothing drags. Rest the pads of your index and middle fingers between your brows, make small, complete circles outward along the brow bone to the tail, lift off, and return to the start; do three passes. Keep your other hand flat on the forehead above as a supporting hand. Then compare the worked side with the other side in the mirror. Stop if anything stings, aches, or reddens.

Before you go further

Why fingertips (and most home tools) stop at the surface

Facial skin is thin. High-frequency ultrasound studies put it at roughly half a millimeter on the upper eyelid and around 1–2 mm across the forehead and cheeks (Ha et al., Plast Reconstr Surg, 2005). A light fingertip stroke mostly moves that top ~2 mm layer, which is exactly why it feels pleasant and is safe to learn on.

~0.5–2 mmSkin: what light fingertips mostly move
Expression musclesOrbicularis oculi, corrugator, frontalis
Further backGalea aponeurotica, suboccipitals, masseter

If the tightness comes back within about 30 minutes, the pull usually isn’t coming from the skin you’re stroking. In Yu-Ting’s classes, tension in the face and around the eyes most often traces back to the back and sides of the head: the galea aponeurotica (the firm sheet over the top of the skull), the suboccipital muscles at the base of the skull that hold the head forward, and the masseter and temporalis that tug up along the side of the head toward the temple. Pressing harder on the surface won’t reach them; it only drags thin skin. That is why the full method works in a set order rather than rubbing one spot longer.

Frequent headaches with a tight band around the head are worth discussing with a doctor; the NHS guide to tension headaches explains when to get help.

Straight talk

What can’t bojin do?

Bojin is not medical care and does not treat any condition. It will not remove wrinkles, erase under-eye bags, or do what Botox or filler does, and it is not a stronger form of gua sha. What some people report is that a few comfortable minutes leave the face feeling less held and looking more rested, temporarily; that is a personal experience, not proof of a structural change. Skip it on broken, sunburned, or inflamed skin, after recent injections or facial surgery, and ask your doctor first if you take blood thinners.

Common questions

Bojin questions women ask most

Is bojin just gua sha with a different name?

No. Gua sha usually sweeps a flat stone over a broad area; bojin works one small spot at a time with a rounded steel tip, short complete circles, and a supporting hand. You can learn bojin’s sequence with a gua sha stone you already own, but the two practices are not interchangeable, and gua sha research does not automatically apply to bojin.

Is “bo jin” or “butin gua” the same thing as bojin?

Yes, those are spelling and hearing variants of the same word, 撥筋 (bō jīn). Bo jin is the usual romanization. “Butin gua” is how it often sounds to English speakers, and it has no connection to gua sha beyond the sound. On this site, bojin always means the face-and-neck method taught by Yu-Ting Lan.

Do I need a bojin stick, and what should it look like?

Not to start. Fingertips teach direction and comfort first. If you buy one later, look for polished stainless steel, about 11 cm long, with a broad smooth edge and a small rounded tip (roughly 3–5 mm) and no sharp seams. The tool cannot choose placement or pressure for you; see the tool buying guide.

Is bojin safe? Who should skip it?

Done with comfortable pressure, most healthy adults find it gentle. Skip it on broken, sunburned, or inflamed skin and during a rosacea flare; wait after Botox, filler, or facial surgery for as long as your provider advises; and ask your doctor first if you take blood thinners or bruise easily. Never use the tip on the eyelid or eyeball. Pain, bruising, or numbness means stop. More in the safety guide.

How often should I do bojin, and for how long?

The beginner plan used in Yu-Ting’s classes is about a minute per zone, two or three times a week, with rest days between. That is a teaching arrangement, not a clinically established dose. Skip a session if your skin is tender, and judge by comfort and a side-by-side mirror comparison rather than by redness or soreness. See the frequency guide.
